How Does the Anime Industry Exploit Animators While Making Billions?

The hidden cost behind your favorite anime

We love anime. We binge entire seasons in a weekend, follow our favorite studios, and celebrate when new episodes drop. But behind every beautifully animated fight scene and emotional moment is a darker reality: the artists creating these shows are barely surviving. This website explores how the anime industry has built a multi-billion dollar empire on the backs of exploited workers, and what we can do about it.

Featured clip: MAPPA animators speak out.

The Brutal Reality of Anime Production

The anime industry is booming. Global streaming platforms like Netflix and Crunchyroll have turned anime into a worldwide phenomenon worth billions of dollars. Yet the people actually drawing, coloring, and animating these shows, the backbone of the entire industry, are living in poverty.

Starting animators in Japan earn approximately $200-$300 per month. That's below the poverty line in one of the world's most expensive countries. Unlike salaried positions, animators are paid per frame or per cut, not per hour. This means they're incentivized to work as fast as possible just to make rent, sacrificing quality, sleep, and their health.

The working conditions are equally devastating. Ten to eighteen hours workdays are standard. Some animators sleep at their desks. The pressure and exhaustion have led to cases of karoshi, the Japanese term for death from overwork. And when workers burn out or quit, studios simply hire the next wave of young, passionate artists willing to sacrifice everything for their dream.

Case Study: Jujutsu Kaisen

One of the most talked about examples comes from Jujutsu Kaisen, produced by studio MAPPA. A particularly impressive fight scene, one that went viral online and was praised by fans worldwide, took an animator 10 hours to complete. The scene lasted just 3 seconds in the final episode. The Human Cost

Behind every statistic is a real person with dreams, talent, and bills to pay. These are some of their stories.

01

The Disappearing Season

Back in 2021, I was excited to hear that one of my favorite anime was getting a new season in 2023. Till this day, that season still hasn't been released. There's been no official announcement, no explanation, just silence. It makes you wonder: did the animators quit? Did the studio run out of people willing to work for poverty wages? How many other shows are stuck in limbo because the people making them simply can't afford to continue?

02

First Year in the Industry

An animator shared their first-year earnings publicly: less than $10,000 for an entire year of work. They described living in a tiny apartment, surviving on instant noodles, and questioning whether their dream was worth slowly destroying their health. Many animators work second jobs just to afford rent. Others move back in with their parents. Some quit the industry entirely within the first few years, their passion crushed by the brutal reality of the business.

03

The MAPPA Situation

Studio MAPPA has become notorious in the animation community for its treatment of workers. Despite producing some of the most popular and profitable anime of recent years, Attack on Titan: Final Season, Jujutsu Kaisen, and Chainsaw Man, reports from animators paint a picture of relentless crunch, impossible deadlines, and poor compensation. Some animators have spoken out on social media about the unsustainable working conditions, risking their careers to warn others.

Why Does This Keep Happening?

Passion Exploitation

The anime industry operates under the term “passion exploitation.” They understand that there are thousands of aspiring artists who want to work in the anime industry. They have spent all of these years growing up and practicing draws from these anime series that they've always dreamed of being part of. This passion becomes a weapon used against them.

Also, because there are many ready workers, the studios can offer low wages and get plenty of applicants. Aspiring animators accept poverty level pay because they're told it's "paying their dues" or that it's a necessary step toward their dream career. By the time they realize the system is broken, that even experienced animators struggle financially, they've already invested years of their lives. Walking away means admitting that sacrifice was for nothing.

The Production Committee System

In Japan, animation series are produced using something called "production committees," where all the companies financing the series share profits. These companies include publishers, toy makers, online streaming services, and record companies. The animation studio itself is often just a contractor hired to produce the actual show.

This system creates a major problem: the studio receives a fixed fee for production, regardless of how successful the anime becomes. It means that if any series becomes so popular that it makes money through merchandise and license fees, the animation company and the artist fail to enjoy any of it. The committee members get all the benefits while the artist feels underappreciated.

Streaming Platforms: Help or Harm?

Global online streaming services such as Netflix, Crunchyroll, and Disney+ are double-edged swords. They increase anime exposure and financial gain, but the financial gain does not always go to the people who need it most.

Streaming services negotiate licensing deals with production committees or distributors, not directly with studios. They also demand more content, faster production schedules, and exclusive releases. This increased demand puts additional pressure on an already strained workforce. Studios take on more projects to stay competitive, which means animators face even tighter deadlines and longer hours, all for the same inadequate pay.

Where Does All The Money Go?

To understand why animators are paid so little despite anime being a multi-billion dollar industry, we need to follow the money. The economics of anime production reveal a system designed to enrich everyone except the people actually creating the content.

1. The 13% Problem: Studios Get Almost Nothing

In 2023, Japan's anime market was worth ¥3.3 trillion (about $25 billion). Production studios; the companies that actually make the anime, received only ¥427.2 billion, which is just 13% of the total market. The other 87% went to merchandising, distribution, advertising agencies, and production committee members who own the intellectual property rights.

Consistently, a little over 10% of the total industry's revenue is made by the studios. The remaining 90% is generated through licensing; toys, video games, clothing, streaming rights, none of which benefits the animators who created the characters and worlds being sold.

Even worse, as of 2021, 39.8% of anime production companies surveyed had operated at a loss, the highest percentage ever recorded. The industry is flourishing, but the studios actually making anime are going broke.

2. Fixed Budgets vs. Rising Costs: The Netflix Problem

You might think global streaming services would help. Netflix alone earned $2.07 billion from anime in 2023, nearly double what Crunchyroll made. But here's the problem: streaming platforms pay fixed licensing fees to production committees, not to studios or animators.

Animation director Terumi Nishii revealed that Netflix's massive anime budgets don't actually reach staff on the ground, the money stops at production companies. Even when projects go over budget or face delays, studios are locked into fixed contracts. IG Port told investors that losses actually get deeper when there are more production orders from overseas streaming platforms, because production delays cause monthly fixed costs to pile up while the budget stays the same.

Studios can't just "charge more" either. Smaller studios are rarely invited to join production committees and don't earn royalties from their titles. Without a seat at the table, they have no negotiating power and must accept low budgets just to stay competitive.

3. Who Actually Wins: The Production Committee Members

The real money goes to production committee members; the investors who fund anime projects and own the IP rights. These include companies like Bandai Namco (toys and games), publishers like Shueisha (manga rights), advertising agencies like Dentsu, and music labels.

Here's how it works: If a production committee member invests 10 million yen in an anime and the DVDs/merchandise sell well, that company may earn 15 million yen in profit. But the animation studio that made the show? They only receive income related to the fixed production cost, no royalties, no profit sharing, nothing.

Studios are treated as disposable contractors. They receive only about 6% of overseas sales revenue and 16% of domestic sales revenue. The production committee system was designed to spread financial risk, but it evolved into a mechanism that extracts maximum value from creative labor while providing minimum compensation.

Final Thoughts

At the end of the day, I love anime. It's been a huge part of my life, and it's probably a big part of yours too. But we can't ignore that the industry is built on exploitation. Artists are suffering, financially, physically, and mentally, so that we can enjoy 20 minutes of entertainment each week.

If we want the art form to survive, and if we want the artists to survive making it, something has to change. Whether that change comes from studios, streaming platforms, governments, unions, or fans, it needs to happen. Because right now, the system is unsustainable.

The people who bring our favorite characters to life deserve to live comfortably doing what they love. They deserve fair wages, reasonable hours, and basic dignity. Until that becomes the industry standard, we have a responsibility to keep asking uncomfortable questions and demanding better.

What will you do with this information? Will you look into which studios treat their workers fairly? Will you support animators directly? Will you share this with other fans? The choice is yours, but now you know the cost of every episode you watch.

Reflection

💭

Developing the Question

At the beginning, school took over my life. So I wanted to research something I was genuinely passionate about. Seeing a video about how animators are treated made me wonder: "How exactly does this billion-dollar industry exploit animators?" Realistically, I was contributing to a problem in the industry in some way. Then I remembered when one of my favorite anime never released its promised season. That made me wonder if animators simply couldn't afford to continue working.

🎯

Purpose & Audience

My intention was to make the audience aware of the exploitation that goes on behind the scenes of the things we love about anime. I also wanted to prove my points to the audience about the severity of the problem. The audience I was intending to target was college students who watched their favorite anime online without really knowing where the money was going.

🎨

Multimodal Communication Choices

I used three different modes. These three different modes include words, images, and audio/visual.

Words

Words helped me communicate information about money, statistics, and how the production committee operates. Writing allowed me to reference books to back up information about how the industry is structured and to discuss the possible exploitation. A website design helped organize information in a convenient to access manner.

Pictures

Pictures helped in understanding concepts of revenue sharing in the form of "infographics" of the "13% problem," images of workers' conditions, and the "anime" style in black, white, and blue. Pictures allow you to see your concepts in physical form. An example of a picture doing so would be a "pie" graphic of how 87% of revenue goes to "everyone except the creators."

Videos/audio

Videos/audio helped add passion and explanation. The introduction video shows anime graphics with surprising pay rates (17 dollars for 10 hours of work). A graphic explanation of how money travels from fans to artists makes the exploitation more real than a simple statement. These modes suit my audience well since they always want multimedia content. An essay consisting of text only would fail to engage individuals who enjoy watching visual stories. Combining analysis with images and videos helped me educate the audience while keeping them engaged in the content.
